# Flour
Wheat has two proteins: **glutenin** and **gliadin**.
When mixed with water, these link together into a protein matrix called **gluten**.
In leavened breads, air bubbles can form in this matrix and expand, making that bready holy structure.

In leavened breads, the leavening agent is a living fungus called **yeast**.
When yeast releases sugars in the flour, it releases CO2. 
When you heat this up, the gas expands. This is called **oven spring**.
Finally when heated, the **gluten** and **starches** set into a semi-solid form, giving structure, and turning from wet and stretchy to dry and spongy.

However, yeast takes a long time to do it's thing cause it's biological.
Instead, **baking soda** (sodium bicarbonate) is basic, and when it combines with an acid, it breaks down int **sodium**< **water** and **carbon dioxide**. 
This is usually referred to as **quick bread**. 

**Buttermilk** is acidic. that's why it's needed. some recipes call for **vinegar**. 
Someone realised that adding a powdered acid to baking soda would help make things easier. Thus **baking powder** was born. It has **baking soda**, an **acid** (typically potassium bitartrate or "cream of tartar") and some **starch** (to absorb moisture and prevent them reacting prematurely).

However, **baking soda** has some neat side effects too. Most notably the **Maillard reaction**, named after Louise Camille Maillard who first described it.
This browns steak and bread, and produces a ton of aromatic compounds.
This occurs more in alkaline environments, so after adding enough baking soda to neutralise the acid in dough, any extra will increase the browning.

Too little baking soda leaves the dough dense and sour.
Too much baking soda expands too rapidly, bursts the bubbles, and leaves it too browned.

One downside of baking soda is that they need to be cooked immediately after the wet batter is mixed. This leaves less time for **gluten** formation, so it has less bubbles.

If you leave **baking soda**-based batter out for half an hour, you barely get any bubbles.
But it is double acting. It forms bubbles when mixed with water. When heated, a second powdered acid (usually aluminium sulphate) reacts with the sodium bicarbonate. The liquid it's in thickens a bit because of the **starch**. 


For **pancakes**, don't overmix the batter. It causes more gluten to develop and become tougher. This causes underrisen/leathery pancakes. You can leave a few clumps of flour even.

**Buttermilk**: 
Used to be the liquid whey left after cream has been churned to create butter. This was allowed to ferment into a thickened sour liquid, which would keep longer than fresh milk.

Nowadays, it's milk with **streptococcus lactis**. A bacteria, that consumes **lactose** (main sugar in milk) and produces **lactic acid**, causing tartness (sourness), and causes **casein** (primary milk protein) to curl and become thicker.
Instead of buttermilk, you can use **crème fraiche**, **sour cream** or **yoghurt** with milk.
2/3 cup yoghurt + 1/3 cup milk.
1/2 cup sour cream whisked with 1/2 cup milk.
1/2 cup crème fraiche whisked with 1/2 cup milk.

**Baking powder** can be substituted with baking soda + cornstarch + cream of tartar. 
1/4tsp baking soda, 1/2tsp cream of tartar, 1/4tsp cornstarch.
This will not be double acting though, so use it super quick after getting it wet.

**Beating egg-whites**:
When beating eggs, here is what happens:
- **Foam**: **globulin** and **ovotransferrin** unfold, and bond in small groups. The whites incorporate a few bubbles that resemble sea foam.
- **Soft peaks**: The groups of bonded proteins become interconnected. These reinforce the walls of the bubbles.
- **Stiff peaks**: The bubbles get smaller and smaller, and the white becomes smooth like shaving cream. Peaks remain stiff and solid.
- **Breakdown***: The proteins get tighter, squeezing the moisture out of the bubbles, resulting in meringue that weeps and breaks. Some acid like cream of tartar or lemon juice can stop things getting too tight.

**pancakes**:
For flavouring, butter is essential (as always :) ). Also it coats the flour and limits gluten development.
Baking powder and buttermilk are part of the equation.
Eggs aid in hardening, and giving extra lift.

# Buttermilk pancakes

##### Ingredients
Dry mix:
- 1cup flour
- 1/2tsp baking powder
- 1/4tsp baking soda
- 1/2tsp [[Salt|salt]]
- 1/2tbsp sugar
Wet mix:
- 1 [[Egg|eggs]] separated
- 1.25cup buttermilk
- 2tbsp [[Butter|butter]]

##### Steps
- Combine dry ingredients.
- Whisk egg whites until stiff peaks form.
- Whisk egg yolk, buttermilk. Drizzle in melted butter.
- Fold int the egg whites gently. Add to the dry mix, gently. Don't overmix.
- Preheat nonstick skillet over medium for 5 mins. Cover with thin layer of butter (paper towel)
- Make 8 pancakes. 2 mins on each side (bubble on top, brown on bottom)
