While it's well known that birds can "talk", they were usually thought to convey emotion like fear, hunger, courtship, rather than actual meaning. It has long been thought that transferring meaning through speech was exclusive to humans.

**Professor Suzuki** spent 20 years studying **Japanese Tit** birds. He found that they form sentences with [[Syntax|syntax]]. 

##### Setup
He installs birdhouses all over a forest with microphones and cameras, and studies their lives and movement. The birds have a lot of **predators** like crows, cats and bears, but one of the most dangerous ones are the **Japanese rat snakes** because they can attack the nests.

##### Sounds
In 2008, he observed the birds making a sound he never heard before, "**jaa jaa**" (which was odd after years of observation). He realised there was a snake crawling nearby.

He started experimenting by introducing other predators, to which the birds made a sound like "**Pitsupi**". Later he found that this is the general alert call. But every time a snake was nearby, they made the "**Jaa Jaa**" sound.

This still wasn't enough, so he started playing the calls back to see how they react. At "**Jaa Jaa**", they started looking at the ground as if searching for a snake. 

This could still just mean "predators on the ground", so he went further. He tried inducing [[Mental misidentification|mental misidentification]] in the birds by playing **Jaa Jaa** and pulling a stick up a tree. If the bird starts approaching the stick to confirm whether it's a snake or not, it worked.

He tried the experiment with "**pitsupi**" where the bird didn't approach. He changed the way the stick moves. After a lot of iteration, he confirmed: "**jaa jaa**" refers specifically to snakes.

##### Grammar
Some sounds are used in combination. For example, "**Pitsupi**" means danger, and "**JiJiJiJi**" means recruiting others. These birds alone don't stand a chance against larger predator birds like a shrike. But together, they can chase it off. In these cases, "**Pitsupi JiJiJiJi**" or "alert & gather" is used.

Experiments show that when a model shrike is placed, "**Pitsupi JiJiJiJi**" makes these birds gather around it. However, "**JiJiJiJi Pitsupi**" doesn't cause the same effect.

##### Language development
Chicks usually leave the nest at around day 18 even though they develop the ability to fly at round day 16. However, as a reaction to the "**Jaa Jaa**" call, the chicks can evacuate the nest earlier. 

In response to "**Pitsupi**", they remain quiet and duck down to avoid cat paws or other predator's attempt to invade.

They know these words before encountering either snakes or other predators, implying a fundamental difference in how they learn the language. These birds seem to be born with it.

##### Inter-species communication
At a later date, people began to experiment with whether **Japanese Tits** and **Finnish Tits** understand each other. The answer is yes. While the precise pitch of their sounds differ, the structure is the same.

A similar experiment was done with the **Japanese Willow Tit**, which has a different "gather" command "**Dididi**". The **Japanese Tit** understood the command from the other bird, and even applied its basic grammatic rules to it.

The birds sometimes use "**Hihihi**" or "Hawk" sound to chase off crows. Even squirrels react to this.
