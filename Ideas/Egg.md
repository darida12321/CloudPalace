Very versatile and good source of protein. Due to the complex protein structures inside, it can be prepared in many ways.

##### Anatomy of an egg
- **Egg white**: 
	- Mostly water.
	- **Ovalbumin**, **ovomucin**, **ovotransferrin**, **globulin**: Complex proteins that let the yolk set in different textures when cooked, or form a creamy substance when whipped.
- **Egg yolk**: Nutritive source where 75% of calories are stored.
	- Mostly a sack of water with dissolved proteins.
	- Larger masses of proteins connected with **lecithin**.

##### Cooking egg
**Egg white**:
- 55C-60C: The curled up proteins in the white uncoil.
- 60C: **Ovotransferrin** starts bonding with itself, creating a milky jelly-like substance. 
- 70C: The **ovotransferrin** created an opaque soft solid.
- 85C: **Ovalbumin** will cross-link and solidify the yolk, producing a tender white.
- 85C+: The egg gets more dry, firm and rubbery. Eventually hydrogen-sulfide is produced.

**Egg yolk**:
- 63C: The proteins denature, thickening the yolk.
- 70C: Starts to form a solid that can be cut. Still fudge-like, colour is dark and translucent. 
- 70C-75CL: Suddenly becomes crumbly and pale-yellow.
- 75C+: The sulfur in the white and the iron in the yolk produce a green  ferrous sulfide.

##### Beating an egg white
As you beat egg whites, here is what happens:
- **Foam**: **globulin** and **ovotransferrin** unfold, and bond in small groups. The whites incorporate a few bubbles that resemble sea foam.
- **Soft peaks**: The groups of bonded proteins become interconnected. These reinforce the walls of the bubbles.
- **Stiff peaks**: The bubbles get smaller and smaller, and the white becomes smooth like shaving cream. Peaks remain stiff and solid.
- **Breakdown***: The proteins get tighter, squeezing the moisture out of the bubbles, resulting in meringue that weeps and breaks. Some acid like cream of tartar or lemon juice can stop things getting too tight.


##### Categorization
1. **Size** is one of the most important factors. Modern lab-chicken mostly produce medium and up. The most common is large.
	- **Jumbo** (71g / 2.5oz). 
	- **Extra large** (64g / 2.25oz),
	- **Large** (57g / 2oz),
	- **Medium** (50g / 1.75oz),
	- **Small** (43g / 1.5oz),
	- **Peewee** (35g / 1.25oz),
2. **Firmness** is mostly for aesthetics, but it is useful for some recipes. It depends on the grade of the egg, but they also get more watery with time.
	- **AA**: Firm whites and tall-standing yolk.
	- **A**: In-between.
	- **B**: Watery whites and flat yolk.
3. **Age** also affects the firmness, and it makes the eggs more alkaline which means looser, wetter foams. Add a pinch of acidic tartar to counteract this.
	- In **America**, the **expiration date** is **45 days** after packing (**Julian date**), which can be **30 days** after laying.
	- In **Britain**, the **best before date** can be no more than **28 days** after laying.
	- Eggs take in air over time. The more it floats in water, the older it is.
4. **Labels** refer to how the chicken was raised. It's basically meaningless marketing. These have no effect on the taste, but the colour can be more vibrant for healthier chicken.
	- **Natural**: The egg hasn't been processed. Eggs are not processed in general.
	- **Free-range**, **free-roaming**, **cage-free**: The chickens are in warehouses instead of battery cages. It's better for the chicken, but the labels have no legal connotation.
	- **Certified organic**: They are free range and not fed animal by-products/pesticides/etc.
	- **Certified humane**: The chickens have more free space. Forced molting (starving them to start a laying cycle) is not allowed.
	- **Omega-3-Enriched**: Chicken are fed omega-3 fatty acids.
5. **Pasteurized**: Heated at about 55C. Kills bacteria, but doesn't cook the egg. It's slightly safer if you want to eat it raw, but these eggs are less firm, and slower to whisk into peaks.
